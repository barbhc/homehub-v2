Five complete flows for adding an item, drawn screen by screen. They all start the same way — brand and model, typed — and they all promise the same two things. What differs is when those two things arrive, and how much the flow asks for on the way.

Why five and not one fix

Three of the six reports were reports you had already made.

HH‑107 is HH‑73. HH‑110 is HH‑63. HH‑111 is HH‑96. Each was closed the first time by adding a warning, collapsing a section, or moving a field to another screen — and in each case the thing that actually happened to the user stayed the same. The search still offered a parts list. The first screen was still long once you opened it. The purchase fields still weren’t where someone thinks about warranty.

So this isn’t a patch. Every route below is a complete architecture, and each one makes a different bet about the same two questions: how little can the flow ask before the item exists, and what happens during the four minutes it takes to read a manual.

Route A

Two fields, then out

The bet: the wizard’s only job is to identify the thing and get its manual. Nothing else is asked, ever — records live on the item page, where there is finally something to attach them to.

Two screens, four taps, no optional fields anywhere in the flow. The identity card resolves in place on screen 1, so the payoff arrives before you leave the first screen. The manual search is demoted to one line beneath the drop zone, which is what the code already claims it wants.

Confirm, then collect

The bet: the moment we correctly identify someone’s appliance is the most persuasive thing this product does, and burying it in a card wastes it. Give it its own screen — then earn one honest, skippable ask.

C

Route C

Watch it read

The bet: reading a manual for someone is the product, and hiding it behind a hand-off means most people never witness the one thing that would make them keep the app. Put it on screen — then ask only for what no document could ever have told us.

The only route where payoff 2 happens in front of the user. It is also the only one that asks for records last, and asks for four fields instead of eight, because by then the manual has filled in category, type and specs.

The risk is honest: four minutes is a long time to hold a phone. The exit is on screen the entire time.

Route D

One screen that grows

The bet: the flow feels long because it is a flow. Delete the wizard. One screen that reveals the next thing as you finish the last, and a single button that is live from the moment we have enough.

No stepper, no Back, no Next, nothing to lose your place in. The three states below are one screen at three moments — the user never navigates. It is the biggest structural departure and the shortest path to an item.

Route E

The item exists in ten seconds

The bet: even the manual is too much to ask for up front. The wizard creates the item and stops. Everything that makes the item useful — manual, room, records — becomes a short list on the item’s own page that never blocks and never expires.

This is the only route where somebody can add six appliances in two minutes and come back to them later. It is also the only one where payoff 2 is genuinely optional, which is its risk: an item with no manual has no upkeep, and the checklist has to be persuasive enough to get tapped.

The branch you asked for

When we can’t fill it in confidently

The rule: every route above assumes the lookup finds the product and the manual is a real manual. When either is uncertain, the flow says so plainly and asks — it never guesses, and it never shows a result it has already judged badly.

These four screens drop into any of the five routes at the point where the confident version would have moved on. That is the whole reason to design them separately: the happy path shouldn’t carry the weight of the unhappy one.

If you want one

C for the flow, D for the first screen.

You named the activation metric as seeing the parsed information of the correct item. C is the only route where that actually happens in front of the user — every other route hands the moment to a notification and hopes they come back. It also asks the fewest questions, and asks them last, when the manual has already answered most of them.

Its risk is real and I would not paper over it: nobody watches a progress bar for four minutes. That is why C’s read screen has to earn the wait with findings that arrive one at a time, and why the exit is on screen throughout — leaving must feel like a choice, not a failure.

What I’d take from D is the opening: one screen that grows, no stepper, button live the moment we have enough. Combine them and the flow is type two things → attach the manual → watch it read → one review, with the four unknowable fields offered at the end and skippable.
